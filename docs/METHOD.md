# The repair loop — a method you can use with or without this app

Performance Powerhouse is software, but the idea underneath it is a study method. This page
explains that method on its own, so any G.C.E. A/L student can use it with a notebook and a pen.
The app only automates the bookkeeping.

> **What this page is.** A description of how the app's rules work and why they were chosen.
> The intervals and thresholds below are the app's own design defaults, not numbers proven to
> be best for every student. The background reading at the end is the general research the
> method draws on; it does not test this app.

## The one question

> **What is costing me marks, and have I actually fixed it?**

Most revision time goes to activity that feels productive: re-reading notes, collecting
papers, rewriting timetables. None of it answers that question. The loop below does.

```text
Attempt → Mark → Diagnose → Repair → Closed-book reattempt → Fresh transfer → Delayed retest
```

## The seven steps

| Step | What you do | The rule that keeps it honest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Attempt** | Answer real exam-style questions closed-book, under a real time limit. | No notes, no Google, no AI during the attempt. Otherwise you are measuring the notes, not yourself. |
| **2. Mark** | Mark strictly against the scheme. Record raw marks, how confident you felt, and how many answers were guesses. | A correct guess is not a secure mark. Count it separately (*trusted marks* = marks you could defend). |
| **3. Diagnose** | Name the **one** main reason marks were lost (see the error families below). | One reason, not five. A vague diagnosis produces a vague repair. |
| **4. Repair** | Fix the smallest thing that actually failed: one definition, one method step, one misconception. | Do not restart a whole chapter because of one narrow error. |
| **5. Closed-book reattempt** | Answer the *same* question again from memory. | This only proves you can reproduce today's fix. It is not yet proof of learning. |
| **6. Fresh transfer** | A few days later, answer a *different* question that tests the same idea. | A new question, so you cannot pass by remembering the previous marking scheme. |
| **7. Delayed retest** | About a week after the mistake, test the idea again with a fresh question. | Only a clean pass after a delay closes the loop. |

### What counts as a clean pass

A scheduled check is closed **only** when the attempt has:

- full raw marks, **and**
- full trusted marks (nothing you could not defend), **and**
- zero guessed items, **and**
- no error family recorded.

A pass with one lucky guess does not close the check. A failed check stays open, and the next
clean pass closes it. One attempt can close at most one check.

### Default timing

| Check | When | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fresh transfer | 3 days after the original mistake | Can you apply the repair to a question you have not seen? |
| Delayed retest | 7 days after the original mistake | Does it still hold once it is no longer fresh? |

The app lets you change both intervals. Doing a check *early* does not count: a retest done the
day after the mistake mostly measures short-term memory.

## The eight error families

Pick the **dominant** one for each attempt that lost marks.

| Code | Family | Typical sign | Typical repair |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| K | Knowledge gap | You never learned the fact or definition. | Learn it, then write it from memory. |
| C | Conceptual model | You knew the words but the mental picture was wrong. | Rebuild the idea with a diagram or a worked contrast. |
| R | Retrieval failure | You knew it once but could not recall it under test. | More closed-book recall, spaced over days. |
| M | Method / calculation | Right idea, wrong steps or arithmetic. | Re-derive the method; drill the failing step. |
| P | Presentation | Correct content, marks lost to units, labels or missing scheme points. | Compare against the marking scheme; make a checklist. |
| T | Timing / selection | Ran out of time or chose a poor question order. | Timed sections; a fixed question-order rule. |
| A | Attention / misreading | Misread the question or skipped a word like *not*. | Underline the command word before answering. |
| G | Lucky / unsupported guess | Got it right (or wrong) without a reason you could defend. | Treat it as unknown and repair the underlying idea. |

## Running it on paper

One line per attempt is enough:

| Date | Subject · topic | Type | Marks | Trusted | Guesses | Conf. | Error | Repair rule (one sentence) | Transfer due | Retest due |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 03 Mar | Physics · circular motion | Topic Q | 6/10 | 5 | 1 | High | C | Centripetal force is the *net* inward force, not an extra force. | 06 Mar | 10 Mar |

Each week, look for:

1. **The topic that lost the most marks.** That is your next repair, not the topic you like.
2. **High-confidence wrong answers.** These are the most dangerous: you would not revise them on
   your own.
3. **Overdue checks.** Clear these before starting anything new.

## Two rules that protect the method

- **Planning is not progress.** If you have not produced one marked attempt today, you are not
  allowed to redesign your timetable. (The app enforces this as the *Execution Lock*.)
- **AI belongs after the attempt.** Use it to explain a marked mistake, never to produce the
  first answer.

## What this method does not do

It does not predict your grade, Z-score, rank or the questions in your exam. It only records what
you did and whether a weakness has stopped costing marks. That limit is deliberate.

## Background reading

The loop combines two well-studied ideas: practising retrieval (testing yourself) and spacing
that practice over time. Good starting points:

- Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves
  long-term retention. *Psychological Science, 17*(3), 249–255.
- Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in
  verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. *Psychological Bulletin, 132*(3),
  354–380.
- Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving
  students' learning with effective learning techniques. *Psychological Science in the Public
  Interest, 14*(1), 4–58.

These studies support retrieval practice and spacing in general. They did not test this app,
its error families or its exact intervals.
